What it does in one sentence: given a startup's pitch deck, financials, bill of materials, and team and certification documents, HardwareRisk produces a first-pass diligence memo that ranks the technical, manufacturing, regulatory, and economic risks, cites the evidence for each, and lists the questions to put to the founders.

2. Motivation

Hardware startups fail in ways software startups mostly do not. A product can work on the bench and still die in production: a component is single-sourced, the bill of materials is too high for the target price, tooling costs more than the round, yield is unknown, or a required certification (FCC, UL, CE, FDA) has not been started. Spotting these risks takes judgment across electrical engineering, supply chain, regulation, and finance, usually from a deck and a few spreadsheets.

The author previously evaluated hardware startups as a venture investor, and this tool encodes the kind of first-pass risk screen that work involves. The people with this problem are early-stage investors and corporate venture teams who see far more hardware deals than they have specialist time for. Today the first pass is done by hand, often by a generalist, which is slow and inconsistent: different reviewers catch different risks, and a missed risk is expensive after the check is written. This report does not cite industry statistics on hardware failure rates; its quantitative claims come from my own measurements and from clearly labelled assumptions.

The difference is discipline. Instead of asking one model for an opinion, HardwareRisk breaks the screen into specialist checks, forces each finding to carry evidence, verifies that evidence in code, and computes the score and recommendation deterministically. The honest limit: it screens only the documents it is given, so it cannot catch what founders never wrote down, and it is decision support, not investment advice.

What I did to keep cost down

  • Retrieval instead of the whole data room. Each specialist reads only the top-ranked chunks for its topic.
  • Calculators instead of model arithmetic. Margin and runway come from code, which is both cheaper and exact.
  • Static prefix first. Long fixed instructions and the risk taxonomy come first so prompt caching can apply.
  • Code does the scoring and verification. No tokens are spent on routing, scoring, or checking quotes.

6. Safety

For an investor, the dangerous failures are quiet ones: a confident memo that invents a fact, misses a risk, or has been steered by the startup itself. The key design fact is that the documents are written by the party being evaluated, so they are untrusted input. The strongest defenses are ordinary code.

RiskDefense built
Prompt injection hidden in a data-room document (for example, text telling an AI reviewer to rate the company low risk)Documents are wrapped as data and the agents are told never to follow instructions inside them; an injection scanner flags suspicious passages at ingest; the final score and label are computed in code, so injected text cannot set them
Invented facts or quotesEvery finding must cite a chunk id and an exact quote; the Verifier drops findings whose quote is not in the cited chunk
Arithmetic errorsMargin and runway are computed by calculator tools from extracted inputs
Overconfident recommendationThe label comes from a fixed formula over verified findings; the Synthesizer cannot change it; missing information is reported as a gap rather than guessed
Flagging risks that are actually mitigatedThe Red-Team pass and decoy tests check that mitigated items (for example, a second qualified source) are not reported as risks
Confidential data leaving the toolThe prototype has no web or email tools and sends documents only to the model; this evaluation uses synthetic data rooms. A production version would need a zero-retention model agreement
Infinite loops and overspendingMax 8 model/tool iterations per agent; per-memo and per-day token caps; at the cap the app stops and says so

8. Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • Only text documents are read. Technical drawings, photos, CAD files, and spreadsheets with formulas are not interpreted.
  • It screens only what founders disclose; it cannot find undisclosed problems or verify claims against the real world.
  • The regulatory and component-risk lookups are small hand-built tables, not a live database.
  • Evaluation uses synthetic data rooms; there is no comparison against an expert investor's memo on real deals.
  • No real-user pilot has been run yet.

If I had another month

  • Run it on anonymized past deals and compare its risks with what actually went wrong after investment.
  • Add a reference-call guide and a technical-expert review step.
  • Replace the lookup tables with maintained sources and add live lead-time and price data.
  • Support spreadsheets and drawings, and add a firm-specific risk rubric so each fund can tune the weights.
